# Drafting Public-Sector Audit Observations: The CAG 5C Framework for Local Bodies

Audit observations drafted for Panchayati Raj Institutions (PRIs) and Urban Local Bodies (ULBs) demand rigorous adherence to evidentiary standards. An audit paragraph is not a personal commentary; it is an official record that must survive rigorous departmental scrutiny, exit conferences, and legislative review by the Public Accounts Committee (PAC) or the Committee on Local Bodies (CPA).

When drafting preliminary observation slips, Inspection Reports (IRs), or final Audit Reports, state audit officers follow standards developed by the Comptroller and Auditor General of India (CAG). These standards rely on an evidence-based operational architecture known as the **5C framework**.

```
[Criteria: Rule/Standard] ──┐
                            ├─> [Discrepancy] ──> [Cause] ──> [Consequence] ──> [Corrective Action]
[Condition: Field Fact]   ──┘

```

---

## The 5C Audit Architecture

Every observation paragraph stands on five independent, verifiable components. Omitting any single element weakens the legal and administrative validity of the finding.

| Element | Definition | Typical Source Material |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Criteria** | The statutory rule, financial regulation, or scheme manual defining what should have happened. | General Financial Rules (GFR), State Panchayat Accounts Rules, Scheme Guidelines. |
| **Condition** | The factual situation established on the ground using empirical evidence. | Measurement books, cash books, paid vouchers, physical verification. |
| **Cause** | The systemic, procedural, or human vulnerability that allowed the gap to occur. | Absence of supervisory checks, lack of training, personnel shortages. |
| **Consequence** | The quantifiable financial injury, risk exposure, or operational failure. | Excess disbursements, non-recovery of dues, risk of asset encroachment. |
| **Corrective Action** | A direct, administrative measure assigned to an authority to remediate the finding. | Recovery proceedings, disciplinary action, ledger reconciliation. |

## Operational Mechanics: From Working Papers to the Final Para

Drafting an effective observation requires systematic fieldwork before a single sentence is recorded on an observation slip.

### 1. Pinning the Criteria

Auditors must never rely on broad statements such as "financial rules were violated." Identify the exact statutory instrument governing the local body:

* State-specific Panchayati Raj Accounting and Financial Rules
* Model Accounting System (MAS) for PRIs and ULBs
* Rule 149 of the General Financial Rules (GFR), 2017 for public procurement
* Specific operational guidelines of Centrally Sponsored Schemes (CSS) such as MGNREGA or XV Finance Commission grants

### 2. Establishing the Condition Through Test-Checks

The condition must carry forensic precision. Vague narratives allow the auditee to evade accountability during the response stage. Working papers should record:

* Exact voucher numbers, bill dates, and transaction amounts
* Measurement Book (MB) numbers along with verified page indices
* The selection basis (e.g., "during a 100 percent check of civil works vouchers above $1,200 / ₹1,00,000")

### 3. Isolating Root Cause from Operational Consequence

A common drafting error conflates cause with consequence.

The *cause* explains why the administrative safeguard broke down (such as non-verification of quarry leads by a Junior Engineer). The *consequence* defines the resulting exposure (such as an avoidable financial outgo of public funds). CAG reporting standards require both elements to be distinctly stated.

## Field Draft Templates for Inspection Reports

The following three examples illustrate how to assemble the 5Cs into publication-ready audit paras across distinct municipal and panchayat operations.

### Example 1: Works and Infrastructure Expenditure

```
Para 2.1 – Excess payment of ₹48,200 due to inflated lead charges in road work

During a test-check of works records of Gram Panchayat X for the financial year 
2024–25, scrutiny of Bill No. 5 dated 12.08.2024 (Work Order No. 14/2024) revealed 
that lead charges for transportation of gravel were billed and admitted for 25 km. 
However, as per the approved District Quarry Chart, the nearest operational quarry 
is located at a distance of 10 km. This resulted in an irregular payment of ₹48,200. 
This was not in compliance with Rule 28 of the State Panchayat Works Accounts Rules, 
which mandates that transportation charges must correspond to the shortest 
practicable route verified against district schedules. As a result, public funds 
were drawn in excess of legitimate entitlement. The irregularity occurred due to 
the failure of the Drawing and Disbursing Officer to cross-check lead distances 
prior to approving the final bill.

Recommendation: The Block Development Officer and Panchayat Secretary should 
recover the excess payment of ₹48,200 from the contractor concerned within thirty 
days and credit the sum to the Panchayat development account.

```

### Example 2: Municipal Asset Management

```
Para 4.2 – Non-maintenance of Immovable Property Register risking municipal assets

During an audit of the asset management records of Municipality Y for the period 
2024–25, it was observed that the Register of Immovable Property in the prescribed 
Form-V of the Model Accounting System was not maintained. Physical inspection of 
records revealed that 14 commercial properties and municipal plots leased out to 
private parties were missing from local asset accounts. This was not in compliance 
with Para 2.3 of the CAG Guidelines for Financial Audit of Urban Local Bodies, 
which mandates a consolidated ledger of all land, buildings, and civil infrastructure. 
As a result, municipal assets valued at several lakhs face high risk of encroachment, 
undetected lease defaults, and legal disputes. The lapse arose due to the failure 
of the Revenue Officer to institute periodic ledger verification.

Recommendation: The Chief Municipal Officer should immediately complete physical 
enumeration of all civic assets, update Form-V registers, and institute an annual 
physical verification system reconciled against GIS property records.

```

### Example 3: Grants and Cash Reconciliation

```
Para 5.1 – Non-preparation of Bank Reconciliation Statements involving ₹1.17 lakh

Test-check of cash and banking records of Block Panchayat Z for the financial year 
2024–25 revealed that monthly Bank Reconciliation Statements (BRS) had not been 
prepared since August 2024 for the XV Finance Commission tied-grant bank account. 
As of 31 March 2025, an unexplained difference of ₹1.17 lakh existed between the 
closing balance of the Cash Book (₹9.84 lakh) and the Bank Statement balance 
(₹8.67 lakh). This was contrary to the provisions of State Panchayat Financial 
Rules and Central Scheme Guidelines, which require reconciliation by the seventh 
day of every succeeding month. Failure to reconcile accounts creates severe 
vulnerability to unauthorized withdrawals, duplicate debits, and fraud. The failure 
occurred due to absent supervisory review by the Drawing and Disbursing Officer.

Recommendation: The Executive Officer should ensure immediate preparation of the 
pending reconciliations, identify the causes behind the ₹1.17 lakh mismatch, and 
enforce monthly sign-offs on the cash register.

```

## Categorization: Major versus Other Observations

State AG and CAG offices segment findings based on materiality and risk profiles:

```
                           Audit Observations
                                   │
         ┌─────────────────────────┴─────────────────────────┐
         ▼                                                   ▼
  Part II (A): Major Observations                     Part II (B): Other Observations
  - Unfruitful capital expenditure                   - Delayed reconciliation
  - Direct recovery cases                            - Procedural record gaps
  - Intentional fund diversion                       - Minor documentation oversights

```

### Part II (A): Major Observations

Major findings involve direct financial recovery, non-utilization or diversion of conditional grants, substantial misstatements in annual financial statements, or structural failures of internal controls. These observations directly inform the audit opinion (qualified, adverse, or disclaimer) issued in the final Audit Certificate.

### Part II (B): Other Observations

Secondary findings highlight minor procedural non-compliance, brief delays in updating service registers, or documentation defects that carry minimal immediate financial exposure. These findings alert administration to procedural vulnerabilities before they expand into systemic defaults.

## Language and Editorial Standards

A CAG audit observation must be legally neutral, uncolored by subjective opinion, and supported by documentary evidence:

* **Impersonal Third-Person:** Never use "we observed" or "I found." Standardize on "it was observed during test-check that..."
* **Tense Discipline:** Narration of field findings must use the past tense ("measurements were recorded," "payments were made"). Prescriptions and corrective requirements require the imperative voice ("must be recovered," "should be reconciled").
* **Measured Wording:** Avoid speculative adjectives like "gross negligence," "fraudulent activity," or "blatant corruption" unless formal charges have been established by competent disciplinary or judicial authorities. Stick to provable statutory terminology: "unauthorized disbursement," "avoidable expenditure," "non-compliance," or "unreconciled balances."
